# Zazi iZandi Literacy Programme Guide for AI Review of Session Implementation

## Purpose of this document

This markdown file is a structured, agent-friendly synthesis of the uploaded Zazi iZandi teaching assistant guides. It is designed to help an AI system understand:

1. how the programme is supposed to work,
2. what a correctly implemented session looks like,
3. how learners are assessed, grouped, taught, and tracked,
4. what changes once learners move from letter-sound work into blending and early reading,
5. what signals in programme data may suggest strong implementation, weak implementation, or likely implementation errors.

This document intentionally removes duplication and merges overlapping content from the standard guide and the blending guide into one operational reference.

---

## Programme overview

Zazi iZandi is a catch-up literacy programme focused first on **letter-sound knowledge in isiXhosa**, and then on **blending those sounds into syllables and words**.

The programme is built around a few core principles:

- learners must master foundational sounds before moving on,
- teaching should happen in **small groups of similar ability**,
- learning should be **play-based**,
- repetition and consolidation are essential,
- progress must be tracked continuously,
- each group should work at its **own level**, not all on the same content,
- assessment and session records are critical for quality control.

The programme begins with **letter-sound identification** and later progresses to **blending and early word reading** once learners have established a strong letter-sound foundation.

---

## High-level programme sequence

### Phase 1: Baseline assessment
Each learner is assessed individually using a **letter-sound EGRA** on Teampact.

### Phase 2: Eligibility decision
Learners who already know enough letter sounds do **not** participate in catch-up sessions.

### Phase 3: Grouping
Participating learners are grouped by similar level of letter-sound knowledge.

### Phase 4: Letter-sound instruction
Teaching assistants run repeated, short, play-based sessions focused on 2 to 5 letters at a time.

### Phase 5: Ongoing progress tracking
Learners' mastery is tracked with the **Letter Knowledge Progress Tracker (LKPT)** (or Letter tracker for short) and with session records in Teampact.

### Phase 6: Endline assessment
Learners are reassessed to measure progress and programme effect.

### Phase 7: Blending and early reading
Once learners have learned their letters well enough, they begin blending sounds into:
- 2-letter combinations (CVs),
- 3-letter combinations,
- 4-letter words,
- more complex isiXhosa consonant combinations and words.

---

## Core instructional philosophy

### 1. Build slowly and consolidate
The guide compares learning to building a brick wall. If you build too fast, the wall falls because the cement has not dried. The same principle applies to literacy learning: learners need repetition, review, and consolidation before more content is added.

Operational meaning:

- do not overload learners,
- keep revising previous content,
- move gradually,
- prioritize automaticity and confidence,
- do not mistake brief exposure for mastery.

### 2. Teach at the right level
Groups should be working on different letters or reading content depending on their level. The programme explicitly warns that **all groups should not be doing the same letter**.

Operational meaning:

- low groups move more slowly,
- higher groups move more quickly,
- group content should follow learner need, not a fixed whole-class pace,
- teaching decisions should be guided by the tracker and recent performance.

### 3. Positive emotional environment matters
Learners should feel safe, encouraged, and noticed.

Teaching assistants should:
- use learners' names,
- praise effort,
- respond positively to mistakes,
- encourage speaking loudly enough for the group,
- avoid disrupting the classroom,
- maintain a strong working relationship with the class teacher.

### 4. Play-based teaching
Sessions are not meant to be lecture-heavy. Learning happens through games, repetition, and active participation.

## Assessment system

## Letter-sound EGRA

The first step of implementation is an **Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA)** focused on letter sounds in isiXhosa.

### Purpose of the beginning-of-programme assessment
The baseline assessment is used to:

1. identify which learners need support,
2. form groups of learners with similar knowledge levels.

### Purpose of the end-of-programme assessment
The endline assessment is used to:

1. measure how much learners have learned,
2. estimate how much the programme helped them.

### Critical assessment rule
Learners must produce the **isiXhosa sound** of the letter. If they use a sound from another language, it is marked incorrect.

### EGRA administration rules
The teaching assistant should:

1. find a quiet space with minimal distraction,
2. sit next to the learner at eye level,
3. give the learner the laminated card,
4. select the correct EGRA test in Teampact,
5. fill in or select learner details,
6. explain the task clearly,
7. give an example using the last row of the grid,
8. start the assessment,
9. mark each letter correct or incorrect,
10. correct any scoring mistake in the app if needed,
11. allow the app to calculate the score,
12. use the resulting scores for ranking and grouping.

### Timing
The EGRA measures how many letter sounds a learner can correctly identify in **1 minute**.

### Additional EGRA rule
If a learner gets the **first five letters incorrect in a row**, the test ends automatically.

### Eligible vs ineligible learners
The guide states that learners who score **30 or more** on the letter-sound EGRA do **not** need to participate in the catch-up programme.

Operational interpretation for an AI reviewer:
- learners with EGRA >= 30 should typically not appear in active catch-up groups,
- if they do appear regularly, this may indicate grouping or eligibility error unless there is another local rationale.

---

## Grouping system

Learners are grouped into **groups of seven**, based on their scores.

### Intended grouping logic
- groups should contain learners with similar letter-sound knowledge,
- groups should move at a comfortable pace,
- learners who already know enough letters should be excluded,
- if one learner is far behind the rest of a group, that learner should be moved to a more suitable group.

### Group creation workflow
1. assessment responses are sorted by score in Teampact,
2. learners with scores of 30+ are identified as not needing catch-up,
3. the data team creates groups in Teampact after assessment data submission,
4. the teaching assistant views group membership in Teampact,
5. the learner names are written onto the relevant LKPT.

### Important operational dependency
Group creation happens after **all assessment data is submitted on time**. This means late or missing assessment submission can interfere with correct grouping.

## Progress tracking system

The programme uses two connected tracking systems:

1. **Teampact** for digital assessment and session recording,
2. **LKPT** for paper-based learner progress tracking.

## Letter Knowledge Progress Tracker (LKPT)

The LKPT records which letters each learner knows and which letters they later master.

### Step 1: Populate from baseline EGRA
For each learner:
- access EGRA information on Teampact,
- read which letters were correct,
- mark those letters on the LKPT for both lowercase and uppercase forms.

### Step 2: Update during instruction
As sessions continue:
- test letters repeatedly,
- only mark mastery when the learner can identify the sound correctly and consistently,
- record mastery on the LKPT.

### Definition of mastery
A learner has mastered a letter only when they can identify:
- the lowercase form,
- the uppercase form,
- quickly,
- confidently,
- consistently across repeated checks.

Important note:
- mastery can take multiple sessions,
- learners do **not** need to be able to write the letter for it to count as mastered in the LKPT.

### Practical implication for AI review
A learner should not be treated as having mastered a letter after only one lucky response. The tracker is meant to represent stable mastery, not brief exposure.

## Session model

## Session length
Each group session is intended to be **20 minutes**.

The guide emphasizes that preparation matters because there are only 20 minutes with each group.

## Session preparation checklist

Before a session, the teaching assistant should:

1. select the group,
2. choose the letters for the session,
3. choose one game or activity,
4. prepare required materials,
5. prepare cards if using a card game,
6. ensure paper and pencils are ready if there will be writing.

## Materials used in the letter phase
The standard guide lists:

1. **Alphabet frieze cards**  
   Large cards with capital letters, lowercase letters, and pictures.

2. **Zazi alphabet cards**  
   Small cards with a single letter.

3. **Board game**  
   Laminated game board with tokens and a die.

## Session structure during the letter phase

Each letter-focused session should generally follow this pattern:

1. introduce 2 to 5 target letters,
2. include no more than 2 new letters,
3. include 2 to 3 previously covered letters for revision,
4. show the letters on the frieze cards,
5. say each sound clearly,
6. have learners repeat sounds together and individually,
7. flash cards to the group and then to individuals,
8. check that each learner can say the sound,
9. run one game or activity,
10. record the session in Teampact,
11. update judgement about what to focus on next time.

## Recommended letter selection rule
The guide says each session should usually cover:
- **1 or 2 letters most learners do not know**, and
- **about 3 letters they do know** for consolidation and automaticity.

This is one of the most important operational rules in the programme.

### Why this matters
A session that includes only new letters is likely too difficult.  
A session that includes only already-mastered letters is likely too easy.

The correct balance is:
- some challenge,
- some review,
- repeated practice.

## Recording sessions in Teampact

The guide gives a clear session-recording workflow:

1. select the group,
2. click **Record Session** and take a photo to begin,
3. select which learners are present,
4. click **Begin session**,
5. after teaching, click **End Session**,
6. click **Complete Session**,
7. select the letters covered,
8. add useful session notes,
9. click **Sync** to upload to the database.

### Good session notes
The guide gives an example of a useful note:
- some learners have mastered the letters,
- named learners still need more practice.

Operationally, good notes should:
- identify who has mastered what,
- identify who still needs work,
- indicate next instructional step.

Bad notes would be:
- vague,
- generic,
- missing learner-specific instructional information.

## Tips for maximizing implementation

The guide explicitly recommends:

- build a strong relationship with the teacher,
- make sure the teacher understands programme goals and results,
- be consistently present,
- ask the teacher for more session time,
- use quiet or low-attendance periods productively,
- if necessary and appropriate, work outside,
- regularly debrief with the teacher.

### Practical interpretation
Implementation quality is not only about what happens inside one session. It also depends on:
- frequency of sessions,
- consistent presence,
- teacher cooperation,
- efficient use of available time.

## Letter-phase games and activities

The guide frames games as teaching tools, not entertainment only. The teaching assistant should first demonstrate a game, then let learners try.

Below is a cleaned summary of the letter-phase activities.

## 1. Practise writing the letters
**Goal:** emergent writing and letter formation.

Possible methods include:
- copying or tracing from frieze cards,
- joining dashed letters,
- air writing,
- writing on the board,
- writing in sand or flour,
- forming letters with playdough,
- turning letters into characters,
- writing letters on a partner's back.

### Pencil grip support
The guide also includes explicit pencil-holding guidance:
- use thumb, index finger, and middle finger,
- support correct grip patiently,
- correct common mistakes gently.

## 2. Letter writing race
**Goal:** practise writing letters and build speed.

Learners:
- hear or see a letter,
- say its sound,
- write it quickly,
- score points for correct writing.

## 3. Memory game
**Goal:** recognize letters and remember where they are placed.

Uses five matching letter pairs:
- 2 current focus letters,
- 3 previously covered letters.

## 4. Container game
**Goal:** identify letter sounds and connect them to words beginning with that sound.

Learners:
- pass a container while singing,
- pick a letter when the song stops,
- say the letter sound,
- give an example word beginning with that sound.

## 5. Snap
**Goal:** quick letter-sound recognition.

Uses small alphabet cards.
Players match identical letters and say the sound when calling snap.

## 6. Board game
**Goal:** recognize and sound out letter sounds.

Setup:
- write 5 target letters on the board,
- 2 current letters,
- 3 review letters,
- each repeated 3 times to fill 15 spaces.

## 7. Hopscotch
**Goal:** say letter sounds aloud while moving physically.

Two versions:
- simple hop-through version,
- more advanced version using die and tokens plus word generation.

## Criteria for correct letter-phase implementation

An AI reviewer should treat the following as the intended standard.

### Correct implementation usually includes
- a valid group,
- appropriate learner attendance selection,
- session recorded in Teampact,
- 20-minute group-based structure,
- 2 or fewer new letters,
- 2 to 3 review letters,
- total focus of roughly 2 to 5 letters,
- content aligned to learner/group level,
- repetition and consolidation,
- active learner response,
- use of a game/activity,
- useful session notes,
- progress judgement linked to LKPT logic,
- movement along the LKPT order over time.

### Likely implementation problems include
- too many new letters at once,
- all groups covering the same letters,
- teaching content far above group level,
- teaching only review for too long with no forward movement,
- no evidence of consolidation,
- inconsistent session recording,
- missing attendance data,
- missing sync,
- poor or empty session notes,
- mastery marked too early,
- learners who are far behind not being regrouped,
- repeated sessions that do not reflect learner progression.

## Blending phase overview

Once learners have learned their letters and established a solid foundation, the programme moves into **blending**, which is how learners start reading actual words.

## What blending means
Blending is the ability to combine individual letter sounds smoothly and quickly to form words.

Example logic:
- /b/ + /a/ -> /ba/
- /bu/ + /za/ -> buza

## Blending teaching sequence
The guide says blending should be taught gradually:

1. **2 letters (CVs)**  
2. **3 letters**  
3. **4-letter words**  
4. **longer words**  
5. **words with complex consonants / special 2-letter isiXhosa sounds**

### Important teaching principle
The same slow, gradual logic from the letter phase still applies:
- move from easier to harder,
- do not skip steps,
- build fluency,
- only expand complexity when learners are comfortable.

### Important material rule
The guide repeatedly says to use **only the words on the word lists** provided in the guide when teaching blending.

---

## How to teach blending

The blending guide describes a very specific teaching pattern.

### Basic blending method
1. show the letters one by one,
2. ask what sound each letter makes,
3. repeat the sequence,
4. go faster and faster,
5. blend the sounds together,
6. present the combined card or word,
7. continue until the learner can read more fluently and with less sounding-out.

### Additional writing-based blending
If learners can write, the guide says it is **very effective** to have them write the letters, syllables, and words themselves while blending.

Important rule:
- only use this if learners can actually write.

---

## Blending progression in detail

## Stage A: 2-letter blending (CVs)
Learners begin by blending consonant-vowel combinations such as:
- la
- le
- ke
- xa
- na

### Goal at this stage
Learners should move from sounding each letter separately to recognizing and reading CV cards fluently.

## Stage B: 3-letter blending
Learners then move to 3-letter combinations such as:
- ewe
- yam
- ezi
- bam

The guide says this follows the **same method** as the 2-letter stage, but with one more sound added.

## Stage C: 4-letter words
Learners next read 4-letter words, often CVCV forms made from familiar syllables.

Examples in the guide include items like:
- mama
- buza
- vula
- xela
- kule
- nini

### Readiness rule
Before moving on, learners should be comfortable reading combinations of these words.

## Stage D: Special 2-letter isiXhosa sounds
After learners can read basic words, they begin working on complex consonant combinations that function like a single sound.

Examples listed in the guide include:
- th
- ph
- kh
- ny
- hl
- bh
- nc
- dl
- ty
- sh
- tsh

These are taught one at a time, in the same gradual way as single-letter sounds.

## Blending tracking

The blending guide includes a **Blending Tracker Table**.

From the available guide content, the tracker appears to monitor whether learners can read:
- 2-letter items,
- 3-letter items,
- 4-5-letter items,
and later special isiXhosa 2-letter sounds / more advanced reading content.

### Operational principle
A learner should not advance simply because they were exposed to blending content. They should advance because they can do it with confidence and enough fluency.

## Blending games and activities

The blending guide adds new activities beyond the letter-phase games.

Below is a normalized summary.

## 1. Board game | BLENDING
Use the same board-game format, but the spaces contain:
- complex consonants,
- syllables,
- or words.

Learners land on an item and must sound it out or read it.

## 2. Hopscotch | BLENDING
Use a hopscotch grid containing:
- complex consonants,
- syllables,
- or words.

Learners read aloud as they hop.

## 3. Letter substitution game
**Goal:** practise blending by substituting letters to form new words.

Example idea from the guide:
- start with a word,
- replace one letter,
- say the new word aloud,
- keep score.

## 4. Syllable substitution game
**Goal:** practise blending by substituting syllables to form new words.

Example idea from the guide:
- start with a base word such as one built from two syllables,
- swap one syllable,
- produce a new real word,
- say it aloud,
- keep or score replaced syllables.

### Team option
The guide notes that this game can also be played in teams so learners help each other.

## What the AI agent should infer about learner progression

A learner or group is probably ready to move forward when data suggests:

- stable mastery of current letter targets,
- repeated correct identification of uppercase and lowercase letters,
- increasing fluency rather than slow guessing,
- session notes mentioning confidence or mastery,
- tracker progression that is plausible and gradual,
- successful reading of 2-letter combinations before 3-letter items,
- successful 3-letter reading before 4-letter word work,
- successful basic word reading before complex consonants.

### Red flags in progression data
- jumping from weak letter knowledge directly into blending,
- introducing complex consonants too early,
- moving to 3-letter or 4-letter work without stable CV reading,
- groups stagnating for too long without reason,
- learners marked mastered without session evidence,
- word-level work appearing before underlying letter knowledge is secure.

## AI review rules: fidelity checks

Below is a suggested logic layer for reviewing implementation data.

## A. Session target fidelity
A session is more likely to be well implemented if:
- it targets an appropriate group,
- the content is near the group's current level,
- the session includes a mix of new and review content,
- the number of target items is not excessive.

Flag for review if:
- more than 2 new letters appear,
- a session appears to introduce too many items,
- group targets do not align with prior learner status.

## B. Sequencing fidelity
A group is more likely to be following the programme correctly if:
- content progresses gradually,
- the tracker and session records show continuity,
- review is present,
- movement is neither unrealistically fast nor absent.

Flag for review if:
- groups skip obvious steps,
- learners jump stages with little supporting evidence,
- the content oscillates randomly without logic.

## C. Mastery fidelity
Mastery is more likely valid if:
- it appears after multiple sessions,
- notes and targets suggest repeated successful exposure,
- uppercase and lowercase knowledge are both considered.

Flag for review if:
- mastery appears after one exposure,
- many learners suddenly master many letters at once,
- mastery is logged without corresponding teaching history.

## D. Grouping fidelity
Grouping is more likely correct if:
- learners in a group are at a similar stage,
- low performers are not mixed with highly advanced learners,
- learners above threshold are excluded from catch-up.

Flag for review if:
- learners with EGRA >= 30 are in catch-up groups without explanation,
- group spread is extremely wide,
- one learner appears persistently far behind the others.

## E. Session recording fidelity
A session is more trustworthy if:
- it has present learners selected,
- covered content entered,
- notes entered,
- sync completed.

Flag for review if:
- session records are incomplete,
- sync appears absent or delayed,
- notes are empty or repetitive across many sessions.

## F. Blending readiness fidelity
Blending work is more likely appropriate if:
- letter-sound mastery is already established,
- 2-letter blending precedes 3-letter blending,
- 3-letter blending precedes 4-letter words,
- complex consonants come after basic word reading.

Flag for review if:
- blending appears too early,
- advanced word reading appears before basic blending,
- complex consonant instruction appears before basic word fluency.

## Practical heuristics for an AI reviewer

The following heuristics are reasonable based on the guide.

### Strong implementation pattern
- baseline EGRA completed,
- learners grouped properly,
- sessions frequent and recorded,
- each group working at its level,
- balanced mix of review and new content,
- gradual progression on tracker,
- specific notes,
- eventual movement into blending after strong letter foundation.

### Weak implementation pattern
- irregular sessions,
- poor or missing notes,
- no clear link between prior learner level and session targets,
- too much new content too fast,
- all groups appearing to do the same thing,
- inconsistent progression records.

### Possible data-quality or implementation-error pattern
- sessions exist with no attendance,
- content entered with no plausible sequence,
- learners marked mastered too rapidly,
- advanced content before foundational mastery,
- eligible learners still included in catch-up groups.

## Non-negotiable programme rules to preserve in AI logic

An AI system reviewing fidelity should treat the following as especially important:

1. **IsiXhosa-only sound production** during letter assessment.
2. **EGRA >= 30 means learner typically does not need catch-up participation.**
3. **Groups should be formed by similar level.**
4. **All groups should not be doing the same letters.**
5. **No more than two new letters in a session.**
6. **Each session should include review letters for consolidation.**
7. **Mastery requires consistent recognition, not one-off success.**
8. **Learners do not need to write a letter for it to count as mastered on the letter tracker.**
9. **Blending should begin only after strong letter foundation.**
10. **Blending should progress gradually: 2-letter -> 3-letter -> 4-letter/basic words -> complex consonants and more advanced reading.**
11. **Use the provided word lists for blending instruction.**
12. **Session records and notes in Teampact are part of implementation quality, not admin only.**

## Recommended interpretation framework for AI outputs

When the AI reviews data, it should ideally classify findings into categories like:

### Likely correct
The available data fits the intended programme logic.

### Needs review
Some aspects may be inconsistent with the guide, but the evidence is incomplete.

### Likely implementation error
The data appears to clearly contradict the intended programme model.

### Likely data-entry problem
The issue may reflect missing sync, missing notes, or logging inconsistencies rather than true poor teaching.

## Suggested output structure for a reviewing AI

When reviewing a teacher assistant, group, or session sequence, the AI could output:

### 1. Summary judgement
- strong,
- mixed,
- weak,
- insufficient data.

### 2. Evidence observed
- EGRA placement,
- group assignment,
- session targets,
- progression pattern,
- note quality,
- mastery pattern,
- blending readiness.

### 3. Suspected strengths
- good pacing,
- good consolidation,
- level-appropriate teaching,
- good continuity.

### 4. Suspected risks
- moving too fast,
- weak differentiation,
- premature mastery,
- inconsistent logging,
- blending introduced too early.

### 5. Recommended next action
- continue current sequence,
- increase review,
- slow down new content,
- regroup learner(s),
- improve session notes,
- verify sync/data completeness,
- delay blending until letter mastery is stronger.

## Final summary

Zazi iZandi is a structured, small-group, play-based literacy catch-up programme with a clear learning arc:

1. assess letter sounds,
2. exclude learners who are already secure,
3. group learners by level,
4. teach a few letters at a time with a strong emphasis on repetition and consolidation,
5. track mastery carefully,
6. record all sessions,
7. move gradually into blending and early reading once the foundation is secure.

The most important idea for an AI reviewer is this:

> correct implementation is not just "did a session happen?"  
> It is "was the right content taught, to the right learners, at the right pace, with adequate review, tracking, and progression?"
